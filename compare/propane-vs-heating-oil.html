Propane vs Heating Oil: Which Is Cheaper for Your Home?

8 min read · October 2026

Past the end of the gas main, most homes heat with one of two delivered fuels. Search for which one is cheaper and nearly every answer comes from someone who sells one of them — propane companies conclude propane, oil dealers conclude oil. Both are right somewhere. The answer turns on your local prices and on one efficiency gap most comparisons skip.

Take a home that burns 800 gallons of oil a year in an older 80% boiler, and price a new 86% oil boiler against a new 95% condensing propane system at typical regional prices:

In the Northeast, where most oil-heated homes are, oil is about $500 a year cheaper. In the Midwest it's the other way round by about the same amount, because Midwest propane is cheap and oil isn't. The South and West are close enough that your own delivery prices decide it. A single national answer is wrong for half the country whichever way it points.

The Number That Matters: Propane's Break-Even Price

A gallon of heating oil holds about 138,500 BTU. A gallon of propane holds about 91,500 — two-thirds as much. So per gallon, propane has to be about a third cheaper just to match oil on energy.

Efficiency moves that line, and it moves it in propane's favor. Propane can burn in a condensing furnace or boiler at 95% or better, while new oil equipment sits around the federal minimums of 86% for a hot-water boiler and 83% for a furnace; condensing oil units exist but are rare. Put a 95% propane system against an 86% oil boiler and the rule of thumb becomes:

Propane wins when its price per gallon is below about 73% of oil's. With a non-condensing propane unit, the line drops to about 61%. At $3.85 oil that's $2.81 propane — or $2.37 if the propane system isn't condensing.

That's why a conversion quote that includes a non-condensing propane furnace is a poor deal in most places: it gives away the one advantage propane has.

Price It Against a Heat Pump Too

If your oil system is due for replacement, the real choice is often three-way. On the same 800-gallon home at the same regional prices, a heat pump running at a seasonal COP of 2.2 to 2.5 comes out at about $2,950 in the Northeast — close to new oil — but $1,900 in the Midwest, $1,560 in the South and $2,490 in the West, below both delivered fuels. Many homes keep the existing system for the coldest nights and let a heat pump carry the rest of the season, which beats either fuel on its own. Beyond the Price per Gallon

  • Who owns the tank. Propane tanks are often leased from the supplier, which usually means you can only buy from that supplier. An owned tank lets you shop around each fill. Oil tanks are typically owned by the homeowner.
  • Price plans. Both fuels are sold on pre-buy, capped and will-call plans. A cheap summer pre-buy price can make either fuel look better than its winter market price; compare like with like — our pre-buy vs cap vs market calculator shows how each plan plays out.
  • Maintenance. Oil burners need regular cleaning and nozzle changes to hold their efficiency; condensing propane equipment needs its condensate drain and venting kept clear.
  • What else it can run. Propane can also fuel a range, dryer, water heater or generator. Oil heats the house and the water, and that's it.
  • The old tank. Converting from oil means dealing with the existing oil tank; ask whether removal or abandonment is in the quote.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is propane cheaper than heating oil?

It depends on where you live. Propane has only about two-thirds of the energy of heating oil per gallon, so it has to be well under oil's price to come out ahead. Comparing a new 95% condensing propane system with a new 86% oil boiler, propane wins when it costs less than about 73% of the oil price per gallon. At typical regional prices that makes oil cheaper in the Northeast and propane cheaper in the Midwest; for an 800-gallon oil home the gap is roughly $500 a year either way.

Is it worth converting from oil to propane?

Only where propane's price is clearly below its break-even, and only if the savings cover the conversion over the new system's life. If converting saves $500 a year, a quote above about $7,500 won't pay back over 15 years on fuel alone. In the Northeast, at typical prices, converting usually costs more to run, not less. It is also worth pricing a heat pump before choosing either fuel.

Why does propane cost less per gallon but more to heat with?

Because a gallon of propane holds about 91,500 BTU and a gallon of heating oil about 138,500 BTU. Propane has to be around a third cheaper per gallon just to break even on energy, before efficiency. Condensing propane furnaces and boilers claw some of that back at 95% efficiency against about 86% for a new oil boiler, which is why the break-even comes out near 73% of the oil price rather than 66%.
