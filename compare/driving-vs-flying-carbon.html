The Right Comparison: Per Passenger, Not Per Vehicle

Carbon comparisons between driving and flying only make sense on a per-passenger basis. A solo driver in an SUV getting 22 MPG emits about 0.48 kg CO₂ per mile. A flight emits roughly 0.15–0.25 kg CO₂ per passenger per mile (economy class). The crossover point depends on vehicle efficiency and occupancy. Two people in a 35 MPG hybrid produce about 0.12 kg CO₂ per passenger per mile — better than flying. Four people in any reasonable car almost always beat flying on carbon per person. Flying solo or in business class (which allocates more fuselage per seat) is almost always the worse choice.

Aviation's Radiative Forcing: The Hidden Multiplier

Raw CO₂ emissions understate aviation's climate impact. High-altitude emissions have additional warming effects through contrail formation, NOₓ reactions, and water vapor that don't occur at ground level. The IPCC and multiple peer-reviewed studies estimate aviation's effective climate impact is 2–4× its CO₂ alone when these non-CO₂ effects are included. This means a flight that appears carbon-comparable to driving may actually be 2–3× worse from a total warming perspective. This is an area of active scientific research, and the multiplier is debated, but the direction is clear and uncontested.

Distance Matters Most

Takeoff and landing burn a fixed amount of fuel no matter how far you fly, so short flights are far worse per mile than long ones. That phase is roughly a quarter of the emissions on a 500-mile flight and closer to half on a 150-mile one. The practical effect: a plane emits around 430 g per passenger-mile on a 150-mile hop but only about 226 g on a 3,000-mile one.

Driving works the opposite way — its per-mile rate never changes with distance. It only changes with who is in the car. That's why there is a break-even distance, and why it moves so much:

  • Alone in a 20 MPG SUV: flying is lower-carbon beyond roughly 140 miles
  • Alone in a 30 MPG car: beyond roughly 400 miles
  • Alone in a 45 MPG hybrid: driving wins at any distance
  • Two or more people in a 30 MPG car: driving wins at any distance

So the common claim that "driving always wins under 300 miles regardless of occupancy" isn't quite right — a solo driver in a thirsty vehicle can be worse than flying on a fairly short trip. Occupancy matters more than distance for most of these comparisons. The calculator above works out the break-even for your own car and party size.

Practical Carbon Reduction Strategies

If you must fly: economy class, direct flights (takeoff and landing are the expensive parts), and choosing carriers with newer, more efficient fleets (A320neo, 737 MAX, A350 vs. older models) can reduce per-passenger emissions 20–35%. On the ground: electric vehicles on the average U.S. grid already emit less per mile than driving ICE, and as the grid decarbonizes, this advantage grows. A BEV in the Pacific Northwest on hydro power emits effectively zero transport-phase carbon. High-speed rail, where available, beats both on carbon — Amtrak Northeast Corridor emits about 0.06 kg CO₂/passenger mile.
