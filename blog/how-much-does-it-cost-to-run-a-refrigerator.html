How Much Does It Cost to Run a Refrigerator? Per Day, Month & Year

9 min read · June 2026

The refrigerator is the only major appliance in your home that runs continuously, 24 hours a day, every day of the year. That makes it a consistent line item on your electricity bill — even if it doesn't show up in obvious ways the way your AC or dryer does. The question most people have is simple: exactly how much is it costing me?

The short answer: a new refrigerator costs about $73 to $139 per year to run at 18¢/kWh, from an 18 cu ft top-freezer at the low end to a 26 cu ft French-door model with ice in the door at the high end. A mini fridge runs about $46 a year. Old refrigerators can cost far more — the only way to know yours is to measure it. The variation comes down to three things: the fridge's age, its style (top-freezer vs. French door vs. side-by-side), and your local electricity rate.

Refrigerator Running Cost by Type (Annual, at 18¢/kWh)

These are the federal maximum energy use limits that every refrigerator made since September 2014 must meet, for typical sizes (10 CFR 430.32). Most new models come in at or a little below them; ENERGY STAR models use about 9% less.

  • Top-freezer, 18 cu ft (~405 kWh/yr): $73/year — $6.10/month — $0.20/day
  • Bottom-freezer, 22 cu ft (~555 kWh/yr): $100/year — $8.34/month — $0.27/day
  • Side-by-side with door ice, 26 cu ft (~715 kWh/yr): $129/year — $10.73/month — $0.35/day
  • French door with door ice, 26 cu ft (~770 kWh/yr): $139/year — $11.59/month — $0.38/day
  • Mini fridge, 4.4 cu ft (~255 kWh/yr): $46/year — $3.80/month — $0.13/day

If your electricity rate is higher than the national average (Hawaii and California average 30–40¢/kWh), multiply these figures by roughly 1.7–2.2×. A new French-door fridge in California at 30¢ costs about $230 a year.

What "Average Running Wattage" Actually Means

The wattage on a refrigerator's rating plate is the draw while the compressor is actively running — not what it averages. Refrigerators don't run continuously. The compressor cycles on and off to maintain temperature, running roughly 30–50% of the time under normal conditions.

That's why "average running wattage" is the meaningful figure for cost calculations. A new 18 cu ft top-freezer that uses 405 kWh a year averages only about 46 watts; a large French-door model about 88. Multiplying a running wattage by 24 hours — a common shortcut — overstates a fridge's use by two to three times. The yellow EnergyGuide label gives the annual kWh directly, which is the number to use.

The most precise way to find your fridge's actual consumption: plug it into a plug-in energy monitor (around $15–25 at hardware stores) and measure over 24–48 hours. This captures the full cycle pattern and gives you a real kWh figure to work from.

How Much Does It Cost to Run an Old Refrigerator?

This is where the numbers get significant. Refrigerator efficiency has improved dramatically over the past 25 years, driven by federal appliance standards that have tightened multiple times since 2001. ENERGY STAR estimates that a refrigerator over 15 years old costs around $95 a year more to run than a certified model — but the spread among old fridges is enormous, from nearly as good as new to several times worse, depending on the model and the state of its door seal and compressor.

That's why measuring beats guessing. If a plug-in meter shows your old fridge using 3 kWh a day (about 1,100 kWh a year), a new top-freezer at around 405 kWh saves about $124 a year at 18¢ — a $900 fridge pays for itself in about seven years. If it shows 1.5 kWh a day, it's already close to a new model and replacing it won't pay on electricity. Our refrigerator calculator does this comparison with your own reading.

If you have a second refrigerator in the garage or basement — a common situation in American homes — the case for unplugging it is even clearer. A second fridge costs the same to run whether it holds a week of groceries or six cans of soda, and old second fridges are often the least efficient appliance in the house. ENERGY STAR's advice: if you need it year-round, replace it with a new certified model; if it's running mostly empty or holding drinks that could fit in the main fridge, unplugging it entirely is one of the easiest high-impact changes you can make.

Is Upgrading to an ENERGY STAR Refrigerator Worth It?

The answer depends almost entirely on what you're replacing:

Replacing a fridge that measures over about 3 kWh a day: Usually justified. Savings of $100 or more a year pay back a typical replacement well within its 12–15 year life. If the old fridge is also showing signs of mechanical problems (compressor running constantly, ice buildup, temperature inconsistency), the case is even stronger.

Replacing a post-2014 refrigerator with an ENERGY STAR model: Much weaker case. ENERGY STAR models are about 9% more efficient than the federal limit — on a top-freezer that costs $73 a year to run, that's about $7 a year, which will never pay for a new appliance. Unless you need to replace the fridge for other reasons, keeping the existing unit is usually the right financial decision.

Factors That Affect Your Refrigerator's Energy Use

Beyond age and type, several operating factors can significantly change a refrigerator's actual consumption:

Ambient temperature: A fridge in a hot garage (90°F summer temperatures) works significantly harder than one in a climate-controlled kitchen. A garage in summer means the compressor runs longer to reject heat into hotter air, and in a cold winter garage some models struggle to keep the freezer cold because their thermostat sits in the fresh-food section. Check the model's rated ambient range before putting it in a garage.

Thermostat settings: The FDA recommends 35–38°F for the fresh food compartment and 0°F for the freezer. Many people set their fridges colder than necessary, which makes the compressor run longer with no food-safety benefit.

Door gasket condition: A deteriorated seal allows cold air to escape and warm air to enter continuously, forcing the compressor to run more often. Test your gasket by closing the door on a dollar bill — it should require some force to pull out. If it slides out easily, the gasket likely needs replacement ($20–50 part, DIY-installable on most models).

Condenser coil cleanliness: The coils (usually at the back or underneath) dissipate heat from the refrigerant. When coated in dust and pet hair, they become less effective at releasing heat, forcing the compressor to work harder. Vacuuming the coils once or twice a year is a free maintenance step.

Ice maker and door dispenser: These features add measurable energy use. The federal energy limit for a bottom-freezer refrigerator with through-the-door ice is about 170 kWh a year higher than for the same size without it — around $30 a year at 18¢. Measured data from the energy-monitoring company Sense put an ice maker's draw at about 80 kWh a year on its own. If you rarely use these features, turning off the ice maker is a simple way to reduce consumption.

How to Find Your Refrigerator's Actual Energy Use

Three methods, from most to least precise:

  1. Plug-in energy monitor: Devices like the Kill A Watt ($20–25) plug between the refrigerator and outlet and display cumulative kWh over time. Measure over 48–72 hours for an accurate average.
  2. EnergyGuide label: The yellow label required on all refrigerators sold in the U.S. shows the estimated annual kWh. Divide by 8,760 to get average watts, then multiply by your electricity rate to get annual cost.
  3. Manufacturer model lookup: Search your model number on the DOE's ENERGY STAR product database, which lists certified appliances with verified annual kWh consumption.
