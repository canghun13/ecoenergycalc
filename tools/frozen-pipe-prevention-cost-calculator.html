Frozen Pipe Prevention Cost: Dripping Faucet vs Heat Tape

Compare what a freezing night costs to drip faucets, run pipe heat cable, or keep a heat lamp on in a crawlspace or pump house, using your water, sewer and electric rates.

Dripping a Faucet Costs Pennies a Night

A thin trickle that fills a gallon jug in about an hour, the rate Mississippi State University Extension suggests, uses 12 gallons over a 12-hour freezing night. At $6 per 1,000 gallons for water and another $6 for sewer, that's about 14¢. Mississippi State puts it at about 2¢ a night at the rates it used. Even a pencil-thin stream at 5 gallons an hour costs well under a dollar a night.

Rates are water plus sewer per 1,000 gallons. Drip the cold side of the faucet farthest from where water enters the house, so water moves through the whole run of pipe, and open sink cabinets on outside walls to let room heat reach the pipes.

The Sewer-Averaging Catch

Some cities set your sewer charge for the whole year from what you use in winter. Austin, Texas, for example, averages three months of water use between December and March and bills that volume for sewer every month until the next averaging period. If a freeze lands in your averaging window, every gallon you drip is billed as sewer four times over: one-third in each of the twelve months that follow. That can turn a 14¢ night into 36¢ or so. Still cheap next to a burst pipe, but worth knowing; some utilities adjust averages after major freeze events if you ask.

Heat Cable: Set It and Forget It

Thermostat-controlled pipe heat cables from hardware stores run at about 7 watts per foot and switch on when the pipe drops below about 38°F, then off above 45°F. A 15-foot cable draws about 105 watts, roughly 23¢ for a 12-hour freezing night at 18¢ per kWh, and about $14 if it somehow ran around the clock for a month. That's similar to dripping a faucet, but it protects a vulnerable run of pipe in a crawlspace or well house without you having to remember.

Follow the manufacturer's rules: never wrap the cable over itself, cover it with non-combustible insulation, keep it out of walls and floors, and test it before the cold arrives. Roof and gutter de-icing cable is a different product at higher wattage and can cost far more; one Colorado co-op figures 100 feet at 9 watts per foot running 24 hours a day at about 650 kWh a month.

Heat Lamps and Small Heaters

A 250-watt heat lamp or small heater keeps a pump house or crawlspace access above freezing at around 54¢ a night. Use only units meant for the location, with guards, and keep them away from insulation, straw and stored fuel; heat lamps are a common cause of barn and outbuilding fires.

Compare That With a Burst Pipe

State Farm says its frozen-pipe and other freeze-related homeowner claims averaged more than $30,000 from January 2024 to June 2025. Ten nights of dripping one faucet costs about $1.40, and the same ten nights on a 15-foot heat cable about $2.30. The cheapest protection is often just keeping the heat on: the Red Cross advises leaving the thermostat no lower than 55°F when you're away. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to drip a faucet overnight?

A trickle of about 1 gallon an hour uses 12 gallons over a 12-hour night. At a combined water and sewer rate of $12 per 1,000 gallons, that's about 14¢. At lower Southern rates it can be as little as 2¢, and even a pencil-thin stream at 5 gallons an hour costs under a dollar a night at most rates.

Is it cheaper to drip faucets or use heat tape?

They cost about the same. One faucet at 1 gallon an hour costs roughly 14¢ a night in water and sewer, and a 15-foot, 7-watt-per-foot heat cable costs about 23¢ for 12 freezing hours at 18¢ per kWh. Heat cable protects a specific exposed pipe automatically; dripping keeps water moving through the whole line but only while you remember to do it.

How much does heat tape cost to run?

Pipe heat cable usually draws about 7 watts per foot. A 15-foot cable uses 105 watts, about 1.3 kWh over 12 hours, or 23¢ at 18¢ per kWh. Running continuously for a month, it would use about 76 kWh, roughly $14. Thermostat models switch on only below about 38°F, so most months cost less.

Will dripping faucets raise my sewer bill?

Only if your utility bills sewer from winter water use. Cities like Austin average three winter months and use that volume for the next year's sewer charges, so water dripped during the averaging window is billed as sewer about four times over. A freeze night can then cost around 36¢ instead of 14¢. Some utilities adjust averages after major freezes on request.

How much water does a dripping faucet use in a night?

A steady drip uses about half a gallon to 1 gallon an hour, so 6 to 12 gallons over a 12-hour night. A thin stream uses 3 to 5 gallons an hour, or 36 to 60 gallons a night. You can measure yours by timing how long it takes to fill a 1-cup measure: 225 seconds means 1 gallon an hour.
