The Formula Explained

The panel count formula every solar installer uses:

Panels = (Monthly kWh × 12) ÷ (Sun Hours × 365 × 0.80) ÷ Panel Watts × 1,000

Breaking it down: annual kWh need ÷ annual production per kW gives system kW; system kW ÷ panel wattage gives panel count. The 0.80 accounts for real-world losses (heat, wiring, inverter, dirt). In Chicago that works out to about 1,285 kWh per kW per year; in Phoenix about 1,900. That ratio is the number to compare against any installer's production estimate — if a quote claims much more per kW than your city's figure, ask what they assumed.

Panel Count vs. Payback: Why "Cover 100%" Isn't Always the Right Target

Sizing to 100% of your annual use is the right answer only when every kWh you send to the grid earns the same credit as a kWh you buy. That's full retail net metering. A growing number of utilities instead use net billing: power you use the moment it's produced still saves you the full retail rate, but surplus exported to the grid is credited at a much lower "avoided cost" rate, often 4–8¢/kWh.

Under net billing, the extra panels that push you from 50% to 100% coverage mostly produce midday surplus you export cheaply. Using this calculator's defaults (Chicago, 900 kWh/month, 18¢ retail, 4.5¢ export, $3.00/W):

With full net metering, the two systems pay back at almost the same rate, so the bigger one simply saves more money over its life. Under net billing, the full-size array takes more than 25 years — longer than most panel warranties — while the half-size array, which sends a smaller share of its output to the grid, pays back about six years sooner. The 55% figure is an assumption for a smaller array; your real share depends on when you use power.

Two practical takeaways. First, find your utility's export rule before you choose a coverage target; it changes the answer more than panel brand or wattage does. Second, under net billing the levers that raise self-consumption — running laundry, dishwashers, pool pumps or EV charging midday, or adding a battery — are worth as much as adding panels. When to Add Panels Beyond Today's Usage

The exception is load you know is coming. An EV adds roughly 3,000–4,500 kWh a year, and switching gas heat to a heat pump can add 2,000–5,000 kWh. Adding panels later usually costs more per watt than including them in the original install (a second permit, another mobilization, and possibly a new inverter), so if the new load is likely within a few years, size for it now and enter your future monthly kWh above. If it's only a maybe, a smaller system with room on the roof is the safer bet.

How Much Roof Space Do You Need?

A standard 400W panel measures approximately 6.5 ft × 3.3 ft = 21.5 square feet. A 22-panel system — the Chicago example above — needs about 473 square feet of usable roof. For most homes with 1,800–2,500 sq ft of total roof area, roughly 40–60% (720–1,500 sq ft) is usable for solar after accounting for roof orientation, setbacks, and obstructions.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many solar panels do I need for my home?

For the U.S. average of about 900 kWh a month, you need roughly 15 400-watt panels in Phoenix, about 20–22 in Atlanta or Chicago, and about 27 in Seattle, assuming 80% system efficiency. The two numbers that drive the answer are your annual kWh use and your local peak sun hours; panel wattage only changes the count, not the system size.

How many solar panels do I need for 1,000 kWh per month?

At 4.5 peak sun hours you need about a 9.1 kW system, or 23 400-watt panels. In Phoenix at 6.5 sun hours the same 12,000 kWh a year takes about 6.3 kW, or 16 panels. In Seattle at 3.5 sun hours it takes about 11.7 kW, or 30 panels.

Should I size solar to cover 100% of my electric bill?

Only if your utility credits exports at the full retail rate. Under net billing, where surplus power earns roughly 4–8¢/kWh, the panels that push you toward 100% mostly produce cheap exports. In this calculator's Chicago example at 18¢ retail and 4.5¢ export, a full-coverage 8.8 kW system pays back in about 25 years, while a half-size system pays back in about 20.

How much roof space do solar panels need?

A 400-watt panel is about 6.5 by 3.3 feet, or roughly 21.5 square feet. A 22-panel system needs about 473 square feet of unshaded roof, plus fire-code setbacks from ridges and edges. East- and west-facing planes work but produce roughly 10–20% less per panel than south-facing ones.
