Balcony Solar Payback Calculator

Plug-in solar kits can't send surplus power to the grid — anything produced above what your home is drawing at that moment is thrown away. Most vendor calculators quietly ignore that. This one models it, so you see what you'd actually keep.

Why Surplus Gets Thrown Away

A rooftop solar system with a net metering or net billing agreement can push excess production onto the grid and get credited for it. Plug-in balcony systems generally cannot. They're legal precisely because they don't export — the enabling laws carve out a device category exempt from interconnection rules on the condition that the system uses zero-export controls and anti-islanding.

The practical consequence: if your panels are making 400W at 1pm and your apartment is only drawing 150W, that extra 250W isn't banked anywhere. It's simply not produced. The inverter throttles back.

This is why your always-on load matters more than your panel size. Buying a bigger kit doesn't help if your home can't absorb the output in real time. A 400W kit in a home with a 400W baseline can outperform an 800W kit in a home with a 150W baseline, despite costing half as much.

The question vendor calculators don't ask: "What is your home drawing at noon on a Tuesday when nobody's home?" That number — not your monthly bill, not your roof — determines what balcony solar is worth to you.

How to Find Your Always-On Load

Three ways, in order of accuracy:

  • Your utility's hourly usage data. Most utilities with smart meters let you download interval data from your online account. Look at 2–4am on a weekday — that's close to pure baseline. This is free and exact.
  • A whole-home energy monitor ($150–300) clamps onto your main panel and reads continuously. Overkill just for this decision, but useful if you're optimizing generally.
  • Estimate from appliances. Fridge 60–150W averaged, Wi-Fi router and modem 10–20W, cable/streaming box 15–30W, standby electronics across a home 30–80W, plus anything genuinely always running. Add a bit for a second fridge or freezer in the garage.

If you work from home during daylight hours, your effective baseline is considerably higher than the overnight figure — computers, monitors, lights and a heat pump or window AC cycling all count. That materially improves balcony solar economics, and it's the profile these systems suit best.

What This Calculator Assumes

Production is spread across the daylight window on a smooth curve peaking at midday, scaled so the daily total matches your peak-sun-hours input with a 15% system derate for wiring, inverter efficiency, heat and soiling. Consumption is treated as a flat baseline across the day.

That flat-baseline assumption is deliberately conservative in one direction and optimistic in another. Real homes have spikes — a kettle, a microwave, an AC compressor kicking in — that would soak up surplus the model discards. But real homes also have days when nobody's in and the fridge is the only thing running. On balance the estimate lands in a realistic range, but treat it as a planning figure rather than a guarantee.

Also excluded: panel degradation of roughly 0.5% per year, any inverter replacement over the system's life, and electricity rate increases, which would shorten payback if rates keep climbing at their recent pace.

Legality Before Economics

Balcony solar only became legal in parts of the US recently. Standard interconnection rules were written for 5–25 kW rooftop systems and made no exception for a 400W panel on a railing, which effectively made plug-in solar impractical nationwide through 2024.

Utah's HB 340, signed March 2025, was the first US law to create a separate exempt device category, modelled on Germany's roughly 800W Balkonkraftwerk framework. As of August 2026, eight states have signed legislation — Utah, Maine, Virginia, Colorado, Maryland, Connecticut, Vermont and New Hampshire — with roughly 22 more having introduced bills in the past year.

Two things worth checking separately from state law:

  • Your lease or HOA. State legalization doesn't automatically override a rule about attaching anything to a railing. Utah's law is unusual in explicitly protecting tenants; most aren't.
  • UL 3700 certification. This safety standard for plug-in solar was only published in December 2025, with certification testing opening in January 2026. Many kits on sale carry component-level certification (the microinverter is UL 1741 listed) without whole-system certification. If your state's law requires certified equipment, verify what's actually certified.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is balcony solar worth it in the US?

It depends heavily on your always-on electrical load and your electricity rate. Because plug-in systems use zero-export controls, any power produced above what your home is drawing at that moment is simply discarded rather than credited. A household with a high steady baseline draw and an expensive electricity rate can see payback in roughly 6 to 10 years on an 800-watt kit. A small apartment with a low baseline load may waste a third or more of everything the panels produce, pushing payback past the useful life of the equipment. Run your own numbers before buying.

How much does balcony solar save per month?

A typical 800-watt kit produces roughly 1,000 to 1,200 kWh a year in decent sun, but you only save on the portion you consume as it is generated. At the 2026 U.S. average rate of about 18 cents per kWh, keeping 900 kWh of that is worth about 162 dollars a year, or roughly 13 dollars a month. Solar.com estimates a standard 800-watt kit covers 15 to 25 percent of a typical apartment's electricity use. Households in high-rate states like California or Massachusetts save proportionally more.

Which states allow plug-in balcony solar?

As of August 2026, eight states have signed plug-in solar legislation: Utah, Maine, Virginia, Colorado, Maryland, Connecticut, Vermont and New Hampshire. Utah was first, with HB 340 signed in March 2025, creating a category for small portable solar devices up to 1,200 watts that are exempt from standard interconnection requirements. Roughly 22 more states have introduced legislation in the past year. Because the legal landscape is changing quickly, verify your own state's current status before purchasing, and check HOA or lease terms separately since those can restrict railing mounting even where state law permits the system.

Does balcony solar still qualify for the federal tax credit?

No. The 30 percent federal Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D expired on December 31, 2025, so systems purchased and placed in service in 2026 receive no federal credit. This matters when reading older guides and vendor pages, many of which still show after-credit pricing and payback figures that no longer apply. Add roughly 30 percent back to any net cost you see quoted with a credit applied. Some state or utility incentives may still exist, so check locally.
