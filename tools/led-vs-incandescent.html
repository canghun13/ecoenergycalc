Which Bulbs to Swap First

The break-even line for a one-year payback is about 45 minutes of use a day for a 60W incandescent, or roughly an hour for a halogen. Anything used longer than that is worth swapping today, working bulb or not. Closet, attic and crawlspace bulbs can stay until they burn out — the savings there are pennies, and an incandescent used five minutes a day can last 30 years.

Start with outdoor fixtures on dusk-to-dawn sensors and any high-wattage bulbs: a 100W incandescent replaced by a 15W LED saves $16.75 a year at only 3 hours a day.

Halogens and CFLs Change the Math

Many "incandescent" bulbs bought after 2012 are actually halogen incandescents — the 43W bulb that replaced the 60W. They save about a quarter of the energy on their own, so swapping one to LED is worth about two-thirds as much as swapping a true 60W incandescent. Still a fast payback in any room you use daily.

CFLs are different. A 13W CFL and a 9W LED produce the same light, so the swap saves 4 watts — under $1 a year at 3 hours a day, a payback of about three years. Replace working CFLs in fixtures that are on for many hours, otherwise replace them as they fail. Either way, take spent CFLs to a recycling drop-off: they contain a small amount of mercury and don't belong in the trash.

The 2023 Incandescent Bulb Rule — and the Repeal Bill

Since August 1, 2023, Department of Energy rules have required general service bulbs to produce at least 45 lumens per watt. Standard incandescent (about 14 lm/W) and halogen bulbs (about 19 lm/W) can't meet that, so they've largely disappeared from store shelves. A second DOE rule from April 2024 raises the floor past 120 lumens per watt from 2028, which would also end sales of most CFLs.

A Senate bill to void both rules (the LIT Act, S.1568) cleared the Senate Energy and Natural Resources Committee on September 30, 2026, but as of October 2026 it has not passed either chamber, and the standards remain in force. Even if incandescents return to shelves, the numbers above still apply: a 60W incandescent costs about $11.83 a year to run at 3 hours a day versus $1.77 for an LED. A cheaper bulb doesn't change that.

Choosing the Right LED

Match lumens, not watts: 450 lumens replaces a 40W bulb, 800 a 60W, 1,100 a 75W and 1,600 a 100W. For color, 2700K matches the warm light of an incandescent, 3000K suits kitchens and baths, and 4000K–5000K is for garages and workspaces.

Two traps catch people. Fully enclosed fixtures trap heat and shorten LED life unless the bulb is rated for enclosed use. And older dimmers built for incandescent loads can make LEDs flicker or buzz — use bulbs labeled dimmable and, if needed, an LED-rated dimmer.

Smart bulbs use the same LED technology, so they save the same energy per hour on. They pay back only through behavior — schedules and motion triggers that shut off lights you'd otherwise leave on. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it worth replacing working incandescent bulbs with LEDs?

Yes, for any bulb used more than about 45 minutes a day. At 18¢/kWh a $2.50 LED replacing a 60W incandescent pays for itself in about 3 months at 3 hours a day and in 2 months at 5 hours. For closets and attics used a few minutes a day, payback stretches to a decade, so let those bulbs burn out first.

How much does an LED save compared with a 60W incandescent?

A 9W LED uses 51 fewer watts. At 3 hours a day that is about 56 kWh a year, or roughly $10 at 18¢/kWh. Twenty bulbs used that way save about $200 a year.

Are incandescent light bulbs banned in the US?

Since August 1, 2023, federal rules require general service bulbs to produce at least 45 lumens per watt, which standard and halogen incandescent bulbs can't meet. A Senate bill to repeal the rule cleared committee on September 30, 2026, but as of October 2026 it had not passed and the rule remains in effect. Appliance bulbs and a few specialty types are exempt.

Should I replace CFL bulbs with LEDs?

Not for the energy savings alone. A 13W CFL and a 9W LED give the same light, so the swap saves under $1 a year at 3 hours a day. Replace CFLs as they fail, or sooner in fixtures that run many hours a day, and recycle the old CFLs because they contain mercury.
