Your Block Heater

Plug-in times follow Anchorage's "plug in at 20°F" guidance (1 hour for small cars, up to 2 for the largest trucks), Natural Resources Canada's 2-hour timer advice for −20°C (−4°F), and the finding that coolant gains little after 4 hours. Diesel and large engines get one extra hour, up to 4.

All Night vs a Timer

A block heater draws its full wattage the whole time it's plugged in; it has no thermostat. Plugged in at bedtime, a 600-watt heater runs about 10 hours and uses 6 kWh, roughly $1.08 a night at 18¢ per kWh. On a timer that switches on 2 hours before you leave, it uses 1.2 kWh, about 22¢. Over 60 cold nights that's about $65 versus $13, and a $20 outdoor timer pays for itself in a few weeks.

The extra hours don't buy a warmer engine. In testing reported in the 1970s, engine coolant rose almost 20°C (36°F) in the first four hours of heating, and two more hours added only about 3°C (5°F). Most of the warm-up happens early, then the heater mostly replaces heat leaking from the block.

Anchorage's air-quality program asks drivers to plug in at 20°F or colder, for at least an hour in small cars and up to two hours in the largest trucks. Natural Resources Canada recommends a timer set for two hours before you drive and puts the payoff at up to 10% better fuel economy at −20°C (−4°F), and around 20% for a single short trip. Electric co-ops in North Dakota and Minnesota also advise switching on a couple of hours before you need the vehicle; the North Dakota co-op says two to three hours does the job even in subzero weather.

What You Get for the Electricity

  • Easier starts: warm oil flows to the bearings faster, and the starter and battery have less work.
  • Less fuel and pollution: a cold engine runs rich until it warms up. Anchorage cites 50 to 100 times more pollution in the first minute after a cold start than after a minute of warm-up, which is why the city hands out cords and timers.
  • Heat sooner: a warmer engine sends heat to the cabin earlier, so there's less temptation to idle in the driveway. Idling to warm up burns fuel for no mileage.

Picking a Timer

Use an outdoor-rated timer rated for 15 amps or more; a 1,000–1,500 W heater draws 8–13 amps at 120 volts. Plug-in timers with a 24-hour dial or a digital clock both work. Smart plugs rated for outdoor use let you start the heater from your phone when your schedule changes. Thermostatically controlled outlets that switch on only below a set temperature save more on nights that turn out milder than forecast.

If your car sits in a garage, it may not need a block heater at all; an attached garage often stays well above outdoor temperatures. For heating the garage itself, see the garage heater cost calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to run a block heater overnight?

A typical 600-watt block heater plugged in for 10 hours uses 6 kWh, about $1.08 at 18¢ per kWh. A 400-watt car heater costs about 72¢ a night, and a 1,500-watt diesel heater about $2.70. On a 2-hour timer, the same heaters cost 14¢, 22¢ and 54¢.

How long should I plug in my block heater?

About 2 hours before you drive is enough for most cars, even below zero. Anchorage recommends plugging in at 20°F or colder, at least an hour for small cars and up to 2 hours for large trucks. In extreme cold below −20°F, 3 to 4 hours helps; testing shows little gain after 4 hours.

Is it bad to leave a block heater plugged in all night?

It won't hurt the engine, but it wastes electricity. The heater runs at full power the entire time, and most of the warming happens in the first few hours. Leaving a 600-watt heater on for 10 hours instead of 2 costs about 86¢ more a night.

At what temperature should you plug in a block heater?

The common guidance is 20°F (about −7°C) or colder. Anchorage's air-quality program uses 20°F, and Natural Resources Canada quotes its fuel-economy gains at −20°C (−4°F). Diesel engines are harder to start cold, so diesel owners often plug in at higher temperatures; check the owner's manual.

How many watts does a block heater use?

Most car and light-truck block heaters are 300 to 1,000 watts. Small cars are often around 400 watts, midsize cars and SUVs about 600, and diesel trucks 750 to 1,500. The wattage is usually printed on the heater cord or in the owner's manual.
