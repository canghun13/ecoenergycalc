What a New Refrigerator Actually Uses

Every refrigerator sold in the U.S. since September 2014 has to stay under a federal energy limit set by its type and its "adjusted volume" (the fresh-food space plus a weighted freezer space). Those limits are public, which makes them a far better starting point than the usual "a fridge uses 100–200 watts" — a figure that confuses the compressor's draw while running with its average over a day.

At 18¢/kWh, here's what new models at the federal limit use:

Two things stand out. Ice and water in the door costs real money — the federal limit for a bottom-freezer fridge with through-the-door ice is about 170 kWh a year higher than for the same fridge without it, and the in-door ice maker is part of why French-door and side-by-side models top the table. And a mini fridge isn't cheap to run for its size: at a quarter of the volume of a full-size top-freezer, it uses more than half the electricity.

Is Replacing Your Old Fridge Worth It? Measure First

Old refrigerators use more — ENERGY STAR estimates one over 15 years old costs around $95 a year more to run than a certified model — but "old" covers a huge range. A 2010 fridge in good shape may be close to a new one; a 1995 unit with a tired door seal can use several times as much. Guessing from age is how people end up replacing a fridge that was fine, or keeping one that's costing them $150 a year.

A plug-in power meter costs $20–30. Plug the fridge into it for two or three full days — long enough to cover defrost cycles and normal door openings — and divide the kWh by the days. Enter that above with the price of the replacement you're considering, and the calculator shows the yearly saving and payback. As a rough guide:

  • Under about 1.5 kWh/day: close to a new full-size model. Replacing it won't pay on electricity.
  • 2–3 kWh/day: replacing it with a new top- or bottom-freezer model saves roughly $30–$125 a year at 18¢. Worth it if the fridge needs repairs anyway.
  • Over 3 kWh/day: likely a pre-2000 unit or a failing seal or compressor. Replacement usually pays back within the life of the new fridge.

The Second Fridge in the Garage

The most common place for an old fridge to keep running is the garage or basement, as a beer or overflow fridge. ENERGY STAR's advice is blunt: if you need a second refrigerator year-round, replace the old one with a new certified model; if you don't really need it, unplug it. A garage also works against any fridge — summer heat makes the compressor run longer, and in a cold winter garage some models struggle to keep the freezer section cold because the thermostat sits in the fresh-food side. Check the manual for the model's rated ambient range before putting it there.

Tips to Reduce Refrigerator Energy Use

  • Set the right temperature — 35–38°F for the fresh-food side and 0°F for the freezer, as DOE recommends. Colder wastes energy with no food-safety benefit.
  • Keep the condenser coils clean — dust on the coils at the back or bottom makes the compressor work harder. A vacuum once or twice a year.
  • Check the door seal — close the door on a sheet of paper; if it slides out easily, the gasket is leaking cold air all day.
  • Give it room to breathe — a fridge squeezed against the wall, next to the oven or in direct sun has to shed its heat into warmer air.
  • Don't stand with the door open — every opening swaps cold air for warm, humid air the fridge then has to cool and dry.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to run a refrigerator per year?

A new 18 cubic foot top-freezer refrigerator uses about 405 kWh a year at the federal limit, roughly $73 at 18 cents per kWh. A 26 cubic foot French-door model with ice and water in the door uses around 770 kWh, about $139. ENERGY STAR models use about 9% less. Older refrigerators can use far more, so measuring yours with a plug-in meter is the only way to know.

How many watts does a refrigerator use?

A refrigerator's compressor typically draws 100 to 250 watts while it runs, but it cycles on and off, so the average over a day is much lower — about 45 to 90 watts for most new full-size models. Multiplying the running wattage by 24 hours overstates a fridge's energy use by two to three times.

Should I replace my old refrigerator to save energy?

Only if it uses a lot more than a new one. Measure it with a plug-in power meter for two or three days. If it uses under about 1.5 kWh a day, it's close to a new model and won't pay to replace on electricity. Over about 3 kWh a day, a new fridge usually pays for itself within its lifetime.
